01 / Decision brief

Build a community observatory in stages.

The first release should answer “What can I try, what kind of game is it, and where can I find it?” A second layer should help makers understand what interested visitors, what happened when they tried a build, and what to improve next. A third layer can show development progress and AI workflows once their evidence is recorded consistently.

Publish catalogue facts now with their missing-data counts. Keep small-sample feedback, automated comment classifications and editorial work queues in the admin view until they support careful reporting. Do not turn the current catalogue into popularity rankings or a games-market forecast.

Primary outcome

Weekly game handoffs: observed game-destination opens from Playtrace, deduplicated per browser-tab session and canonical project. This measures whether discovery leads somewhere useful. It does not establish that somebody played, bought or liked the game.

Companion outcome

Projects receiving useful feedback: distinct projects with a new, editor-reviewed actionable contribution in the week. Track self-reported successful access separately. More traffic is only valuable if destinations work and makers learn something.

Both outcome definitions are proposals. Game handoff events and a structured “actionable” review label are not collected today. Establish a four-week baseline before choosing improvement targets.

The limiting factor is currently evidence quality. Of 1,296 entries, 771 have a nonspecific genre, 737 have an unspecified stage, and 883 lack device metadata. Fixing these fields improves both browsing and the credibility of every later comparison.

The Reddit evidence queue is the strongest qualitative starting point.

A read-only production query found 637 ready feedback jobs for the 11 August–11 October window, containing 3,090 classified comments. There are 244 cited draft summaries, 157 jobs with comments requiring manual interpretation, and 236 with no eligible comments. None of the draft reviews has been published.

The machine assigned 799 comments to visuals, 761 to general impressions, 452 to idea/design, 89 to reliability, 70 to controls, 31 to accessibility, 28 to challenge, 17 to pacing, and 843 to unrelated material. These counts describe unreviewed model labels, not verified player sentiment. Only 522 comments pass the current composer’s combined probability, theme and tone gates; that threshold has not been calibrated against human judgments.

Use this material to build an editor-reviewed theme index with exact comment links. A useful synthesis might say “Three sampled comments discussed controls” and cite all three. Show disagreements and the sample window. Do not transform sarcasm, questions or an off-topic remark into a complaint about the game.

Sampling limits: up to ten latest source posts per entry, up to 100 retrieved comments per post, and at most 30 classified eligible comments per entry. Thirty-six jobs are marked capped. Creator replies, removed text and known bots are excluded. Comments are not verified play sessions; several comments can come from the same person. The 637 jobs target recently updated entries, so they do not cover recent comments on every older game.

A specific measurement bug to fix before reporting quiz completion: expired unfinished rounds are deleted when another round starts. Dividing current completed rounds by retained starts produces a biased completion rate. Record separate durable daily starts/finishes, with bot controls and a fixed window, before using that KPI.

Enrichment workflow

  1. Resolve the current public project and effective destination, then look for known store IDs and canonical URLs.
  2. Fetch allowlisted public store metadata within cache/rate limits. For itch.io, the authenticated games endpoint covers games the account owns or can edit; it is not a general community catalogue API. Use permitted public page metadata for other projects. itch.io API reference.
  3. Extract name, artwork, build controls, store type, release fields and explicit supported systems. Retain evidence separately from accepted metadata.
  4. Use Jev for candidate labels and comment themes when deterministic evidence is insufficient. Keep the source excerpt, model/version and ambiguity. Send contradictions and low-confidence cases to an editor.
  5. After review, publish a small dated aggregate snapshot. Recheck failed/blocked URLs conservatively; a timeout does not retire a game.

Rules for publishing comparisons

Always show period, source, sample size, question/version and unknown share. For a proposed public response dashboard, initially suppress sensitive/private cells below five and apply complementary suppression so totals cannot reveal them. This is a display rule, not an anonymity guarantee. Existing public contribution text follows its existing visibility/removal rules.

At fewer than 30 eligible answers per compared group, default to “early feedback” and counts instead of rankings. At 30 or more, descriptive percentages can be useful but remain a convenience sample; this threshold does not make them representative. Where intervals help, state assumptions and use a suitable binomial interval for a single proportion. It still does not account for recruitment bias, repeat browsers or people who chose not to answer.

Do not pool questionnaire revisions, quiz pools, private studies and public feedback into one score. Report skipped questions separately. Edits replace current responses rather than adding votes; deletes invalidate derived summaries. Preserve raw category counts for internal QA but release only the reviewed aggregation.

For research reporting, publish recruitment, population, exact question wording and collection method. This follows the transparency concerns in AAPOR’s survey guidance. A survey of volunteers on Playtrace cannot establish what all gamers want.

07 / Better questions, less work for visitors

Separate curiosity from experience.

Keep the current quick multi-select interaction, but add context and neutral choices. “What catches your eye?” measures perceived appeal; “What would help it shine?” alone cannot tell whether the person tried the game. Offer two short routes, save once at the end, allow skipping and retain revision-specific option IDs.

Route A · Before trying

  1. What have you seen so far?
    Screenshots only / trailer or GIF / game page / I have already played. Route played visitors to B.
  2. What makes you interested?
    Idea/mechanics; visuals/atmosphere; story/world; challenge; social/co-op play; not interested yet. Multi-select, up to three; “not interested yet” is exclusive.
  3. What would help you decide to try it?
    Playable demo; clearer gameplay explanation; device support; price/access information; accessibility information; nothing else. Multi-select, with “nothing else” exclusive.
  4. Anything you expected to find?
    Optional short note. Avoid prompts that imply the game failed.

Four questions. Interests are self-reported expectations, not demand forecasts. Public sharing remains an explicit choice in the public feedback flow.

Route B · After trying

  1. What happened when you tried it?
    Played successfully / link failed / page loaded but no build / login, purchase or invite blocked access / game failed to start / haven’t tried.
  2. How far did you get?
    A few minutes / one session / several sessions / prefer not to say. Only ask after successful access.
  3. How did it compare with what you expected?
    Much worse / a little worse / about as expected / a little better / much better / had no expectation.
  4. What should the maker focus on next?
    Instructions; controls; performance; pacing/difficulty; content; accessibility; nothing comes to mind. Multi-select with exclusive none.
  5. One useful thing to tell the maker?
    Optional note; invite a concrete example without requesting personal information.

Maximum five questions. Branching, selection caps and exclusive “none” options are proposed additions; the current linear questionnaire engine does not enforce them. Until then, use two separate forms, an explicit experience question and carefully validated answer combinations.

A failed-access route should ask an optional short description and stop before satisfaction questions. Record the effective game/build URL and its version/date from catalogue context; do not ask visitors to repeat it. Preserve “not tried,” “unsure,” “none” and missing as distinct values.

Use separate, optional small studies for return intent, difficulty or ideal price rather than making every visitor answer everything. For ideal price, specify “this demo” versus “the eventual complete game,” show currency, allow free/unsure, and avoid showing other users’ suggestions before answering.

For an actual expectation-gap study, collect the same neutral feature question before and after a voluntary play attempt under a short-lived study token. Analyze matched pairs separately from all preview and post-play responses. Current retrospectively answered questions cannot provide that comparison.

The screenshot game needs a precise interpretation.

Keep the five-question round as discovery and fun. Call its metric screenshot-name match accuracy. If recognition matters, ask “Have you seen this game before?” in a separate optional study. If title fit matters, show one screenshot and ask about several candidate names without presenting one as the correct identity. Those are different experiments.

Record pool version, image version, answer position and which alternatives were offered; respect image failures and skips. Repeat rounds are correlated, and alternative names have unequal exposure. Do not call a frequently chosen distractor the “preferred title” without dividing by times it was offered and designing a genuine name-preference task.

Use the existing stack, with bounded reporting work.

Keep Cloudflare. Separate operational storage from read-heavy reports: D1 holds current records and constrained contribution writes; R2/static exports hold dated public aggregates; GoatCounter records a small set of discovery actions; Cloudflare Web Analytics remains a traffic/performance reference. Reporting architecture

  1. Daily operator/scheduled aggregation: read changed records using watermarks/indexes; validate canonical aliases and moderation. Aggregate feedback by form revision/build and catalogue data by accepted dimensions.
  2. Separate outputs: a public catalogue snapshot and an admin-only operational/response report. Never put private responses, account favorites, edit keys or raw analytics identifiers in a public R2 object or GitHub Pages file.
  3. Public reports: serve small versioned JSON with asOf, source window, taxonomy/metric version, denominators, unknown counts, suppression flags and staleness. A failed refresh leaves the previous snapshot visibly dated.
  4. Quota controls: cap work per run and oldest pending batch; suspend optional backfills when the account’s observed usage threatens the reserved operating budget. Initial planning budget: optional analysis under 10% of the daily free read/write allowance, subject to profiling and other sites’ account usage.
  5. Deletion and moderation: invalidate affected aggregates/summaries; support recomputation from current permitted records. Avoid publishing very small daily slices that could reveal deleted/private responses by subtraction. Keep an explicit snapshot retention/removal policy.

Proposed retention for new analytics telemetry: keep event-level records only where necessary, for at most 30 days; prefer aggregate-only collection. Reassess after the first month. This is not a new retention policy for authored feedback: its existing editing, moderation and deletion controls remain authoritative. Never reuse guest edit credentials as analytics identifiers.

Three reporting surfaces

  • Public “Community insights”: catalogue mix, build/destination availability, evidenced release events, recently updated projects, methodology and downloadable public aggregates. No small-sample ratings leaderboard.
  • Maker’s project panel: source-linked feedback themes, exposure context, build-specific improvements and opted-in aggregated expectations. Private Quick review answers stay with editors under the current promise; sharing them with makers needs an explicit visibility change and new consent.
  • Admin “Health & research”: coverage gaps, outage/fallback rates, review backlog, link repairs, label accuracy, survey versions and sample sufficiency. Drill into existing editorial evidence without a raw visitor identity export.

First three experiments worth considering

  1. Clear access labels: compare a specific action (“Play in browser,” “Download demo,” “Wishlist on Steam”) with the existing generic visit label. Primary outcome: handoffs among equally exposed eligible cards. Guardrails: access failures, accidental exits, accessibility and image load time.
  2. Genre/mechanic enrichment: measure zero-result filtering and game handoffs before/after a reviewed taxonomy rollout. If not randomized, report an observational change and account for archive additions and traffic mix.
  3. Short contextual feedback: compare one neutral first-impression prompt with the existing two prompts. Outcome: accepted, useful contributions per form exposure; guardrails: abandonment, duplicate contributions and skew toward preview-only respondents.

Do not run these at the same time on tiny traffic. Predefine one outcome, the eligible population, the minimum detectable change and the observation period once traffic is known. Weekly highlight selection is editorial and cannot serve as an unbiased comparison group.

Operating rhythm

Daily: quota/availability, broken links, failed images and stale snapshots. Saturday: validate highlights, inspect search friction and new feedback, assign the next editorial batch. Monthly: publish a dated community report with method changes, known gaps and source-linked examples; revise priorities based on the decisions it actually helped people make.

The owner approves definitions and priorities; editors own classification and public synthesis; engineering owns instrumentation, budgets and reproducibility. Makers can propose corrections, but sources and previous accepted facts remain traceable.

10 / Evidence, scope and reproducibility

What this investigation did—and what remains unknown.

The business-analyst skill and its implementation playbook guided the objective, source audit, grain/denominator definitions, quality checks and phased recommendations. The work used a full published catalogue snapshot, read-only production aggregates, the actual data schema and collection code, and primary documentation. It did not create visitor contributions, rerun paid model classification, modify source records or deploy new analytics.
